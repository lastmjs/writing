# I. The Prison
## 牢 · Nagasaki, the eleventh month of Meireki 3 (winter, 1657)

They came down from the north in the rain, roped together at the waist like a string of dried fish, and Yoichi counted them through the gate because counting them was his work.

Thirty-one. Farmers, mostly. You could tell farmers by their hands and by the way they stood, as if the ground beneath them were a thing to be reckoned with and not merely walked upon. There were women among them, and two old men, and a boy of perhaps twelve who kept turning his head to look at the harbor, where the Chinese junks rode at anchor with their painted eyes, as if he had never seen the sea so wide. Maybe he had not. Kōri was a farming village beside the inland bay of Ōmura, a day's walk north, and its people looked at water the way they looked at a neighbor's field: with interest, but no claim.

"Kirishitan," said Gonroku, the other turnkey, spitting into the mud beside the gatepost. "Ōmura's full of them, they say. They had a cave in the hills where they kept a picture of the foreign god's mother, and a child prophet, and who knows what else. Like Amakusa all over again."

Yoichi said nothing. He was twenty-three years old, the son of a son of Nagasaki, and he had been a turnkey at the Sakuramachi prison for four years. In four years he had learned that silence was the safest thing a man could carry through that gate, safer than a sword or a reputation, and that most of what Gonroku said was not meant to be answered.

He knew, of course, what Kirishitan were. Everyone in Nagasaki knew. The city had been theirs once, before he was born. His grandmother spoke of it sometimes when she was tired and forgot herself: the bells, the processions on the holy days, the great church out on the cape, of which not one stone now stood on another, the foreign priests in their black robes walking up the slope from the harbor. Then she would remember, and her face would close like a shutter, and she would say that it had been a time of madness and the shogun had been right to end it.

Now there were temples where the churches had stood. Every household in the city was registered at one, and every New Year the officials came with the board, and you stepped on it, and that was that. Yoichi had stepped on it every year since he was old enough to walk. It was a wooden plank with a little copper picture set into the middle, so worn that you could hardly tell what it had been. A face, maybe. He had never thought much about it. It was like paying the rice tax, or bowing when a magistrate's palanquin went by.

The prisoners were put in the east cells, the long ones with the lattice of square-cut timber facing the passage. They sat down in the straw without being told. Yoichi went along the lattice with the water bucket, as he did every evening, and passed the ladle through the bars.

Most of them drank without looking at him. One did not.

He was one of the two old men: small and brown and bent at the shoulders, with a farmer's thick wrists and a wisp of white beard. When Yoichi held the ladle to the bars, the old man took it in both hands, drank, and then bowed his head over it, very slightly, before he handed it back. His lips moved.

"What did you say?" Yoichi asked. It came out more sharply than he meant.

The old man looked up at him. His eyes were wet from the cold, and very calm.

"I said thank you," he said. "And I asked a blessing on the one who gave it."

"A blessing from whom?"

The old man only smiled, as if Yoichi had asked a riddle whose answer they both knew, and moved back into the straw.

* * *

His name, the register said, was Kyūemon, of Kōri village in the domain of Ōmura, sixty-one years of age, cultivator. Arrested on the testimony of others for belonging to the evil sect. Unrepentant.

Gonroku said that the magistrate, Kurokawa-sama, was furious. He had been sending reports to Edo for years that the Christians were finished in the lands around Nagasaki, that the last of the priests had been caught and the last of the believers had fallen. Now here were farmers by the dozen, by the hundred if the rumors held, who had been keeping the old god in their villages for thirty years under the very noses of their lord and his officials. It was an embarrassment. Embarrassments, in Gonroku's experience, ended badly for whoever had caused them.

"They'll be asked to step," he said. "Some will. The rest..." He drew a finger across his throat, not unkindly. It was simply a fact, like the weather.

Some did step. Every few days the officials came and took a group of prisoners across the city to the magistrate's office for examination, and some of them came back with their faces gray and their eyes on the floor, and a few days later they were released into the custody of their village headmen with their names entered in a new register, the register of those who had fallen. Yoichi watched them go out through the gate. They did not look relieved. They looked like people who had left something behind them in a room they could never enter again.

Others came back from the magistrate's office carried between two guards.

Kyūemon came back walking, the first time, and the second. The third time he came back carried, with his feet wrapped in rags and one arm hanging wrong, and the guards dropped him in the straw like a sack of millet. That night, when Yoichi came with the water, the old man could not lift his hands to the ladle. Yoichi stood at the lattice for a long moment. Then he put the ladle through the bars, low, and held it to the old man's mouth himself.

Kyūemon drank. When he had finished, he said, "Thank you." And then, after a moment, with great effort: "You have a kind hand, turnkey."

"I have a bucket," Yoichi said. "It's my work."

"Yes." The old man closed his eyes. "But it is your hand."

* * *

Winter came down hard that year. Wind off the harbor, sleet in the alleys, frost on the timbers of the cells in the morning. The prisoners huddled together in the straw for warmth and the sick ones coughed through the night. Three of the Kōri people died before the New Year. Yoichi carried them out himself, wrapped in their own thin coats, and he noticed that the ones who were left knelt as the bodies went past, and touched their foreheads and their chests and their shoulders with the tips of their fingers, quickly, so the guards would not see.

He saw. He did not report it.

He could not have said why. It was not sympathy, or not only. It was something nearer to curiosity, the way a man who has lived all his life beside a river might one day stop on the bank and wonder, for the first time, where the water goes.

At night, when the lamp in the passage was turned down and Gonroku was asleep on his mat by the brazier, the prisoners sang. Not aloud. It was hardly more than breathing, a low murmur that rose and fell among them, the words too soft to make out. Sometimes Yoichi stood in the dark at the end of the passage and listened to it, the way he might listen to rain on a roof. Once, he caught a few of the words. *Santa Maria*. And something like *Deus*. And something that sounded like *ora pro nobis*, which meant nothing to him at all.

"What is that you sing?" he asked Kyūemon one night, very quietly, when he brought the water.

The old man was quiet for so long that Yoichi thought he had not heard. Then he said, "You could have me beaten for it."

"I know."

"You have not."

"No."

Kyūemon looked at him through the lattice. The lamp was behind Yoichi, and the old man's face was lit by it, and Yoichi saw that he was weighing something, the way a farmer weighs seed in his hand before he sows it, judging whether the ground is ready.

"It is called an *orasho*," Kyūemon said at last. "A prayer. We ask the Mother of God to pray for us. Now, and at the hour of our death."

"Your death is coming soon enough," Yoichi said. He did not say it to be cruel. He said it because it was true, and because he wanted to see what the old man would do with it.

Kyūemon nodded. "Yes. Sooner for some of us than for you, perhaps. But it is coming for you too, turnkey. For everyone. That is why we ask."

"You could step," Yoichi said. "Others have. They let them go home."

"Yes."

"Then why don't you?"

The old man did not answer at once. Out in the passage the wind found a gap in the shutters and the lamp flame bent and straightened. Down the row a child was coughing, a small dry cough like a sparrow's.

"There is a question," Kyūemon said slowly, "that is asked of every person once. Not with words. With the whole life. Most people do not even know they are being asked. They answer it anyway, by the way they live. But for a few, God lets the question come in a form that can be seen. A board on the floor. A face. And then the answer is very simple. The feet move, or they do not."

"And yours do not."

"Mine do not." He smiled, and his cracked lips bled a little at the corner. "They are old feet. They are tired. But they will not do that."

"Because you are afraid of your god."

"No." The old man's voice was very gentle. "Because I am not afraid of him anymore. That is the difference, you see. I was, once. Then I learned what he did. And after that, how could I put my foot on his face? He has already been stepped on. For me. I will not be one more foot."

Yoichi did not understand. He did not understand at all. But that night, lying on his mat by the brazier with Gonroku snoring beside him, he found that the words would not leave him alone. *He has already been stepped on. For me.* He turned them over and over like a stone in his hand, looking for the seam.
