# IV. The Board
## 板 · Manji 2 to Kanbun 8 (1659–1668)

The first New Year came, as it always did, with pine boughs at the gates and rice cakes and the sound of mallets in every lane, and on the fourth day of the first month, the officials came to Kōya-machi with the board.

Yoichi had dreaded it for half a year. He had lain awake over it. He had asked his grandmother what to do, and she had only shaken her head and said, *I cannot tell you, I am the last person in Nagasaki who can tell you*, and gone back to her loom with her mouth pressed tight.

In the end it was very simple, as Kyūemon had said it would be.

The ward elder and the two ward headmen went down the lane from house to house, as they did every year, with the register and a clerk from the magistrate's office carrying the board in a wooden box. At each door the family slid it open, and the clerk laid the board on a straw mat on the earthen floor just inside, and the family stepped, one by one, in the order of the register, and pressed their seals in the book. It was the same board as every year: a plank of dark wood, about as long as a man's forearm, with a little round of copper set into the middle. Children were held up by their mothers, and their bare feet set down on it, and lifted away again, laughing or wailing. Old men leaned on their sons' arms. Next door, the dyer's family went across it all six together, cheerfully, in a hurry to get back to their New Year's sake. Then the officials were at Yoichi's door.

Then it was his grandmother's turn.

She walked to the mat with her back straight and her face closed like a door, and she put her foot on the copper and walked across it, and turned and bowed, and the ward elder found her name in the register and she pressed her seal beside it.

Then it was his.

He had never looked at the copper before. Not once, in twenty years. He looked at it now. It was the size of a large coin, rubbed so smooth by the feet of the city that only the shape of the picture remained: a woman seated, and across her knees, the long pale body of a man. The mother, holding her son, after they had taken him down from the cross.

He put his foot on it.

It was cold. That was all. The copper was cold under his bare sole, and smooth as river stone, and then he was across it, and the ward elder was saying, *Yoichi, turnkey, son of Kansuke, deceased*, and holding out the register for his seal. He pressed it. He bowed. The clerk picked up the board and put it back in its box, and the officials moved on to the next door, and Yoichi slid his own door shut behind them and sat down on the floor with his back against it.

His grandmother was kneeling by the brazier. She did not look up.

"So," she said. "Now you know."

* * *

It was the next autumn that she asked him to take her to Urakami.

She had not been out of Kōya-machi in years, except to the temple and the bathhouse. Her knees were bad. But she had made up her mind, and there was no unmaking it, so on a rest day in the ninth month Yoichi carried her on his back out of the city by the north road, past the foot of Nishizaka, where he did not look up, and along the river into the valley of Urakami, where the rice was standing gold in the paddies and the farmhouses sat low under their thatch among the persimmon trees.

"Isabel had a sister," his grandmother said, on his back, into his ear. "Younger. She married a man from Nakano, in Urakami, the year before the board came. I have not spoken to her since. Not once in thirty years. I heard she was still living. I heard things about Urakami, too. Things people whisper. If they are true..."

They were true.

Isabel's sister was a tiny, toothless, white-haired woman named Kame, who stood in the doorway of her son's farmhouse and stared at the two of them for a long time without a word. Then she said, "Marina," as if she had last seen her yesterday, and the two old women held each other in the doorway and wept, while Yoichi stood in the yard and studied the persimmons with great attention.

He went to Urakami often after that. Not too often. There was a careful rhythm to such things, he learned, and Urakami had been keeping it for thirty years. A rest day here and there. A gift of dried fish, a pot of the dyer's leftover indigo, a basket of persimmons to carry home. Nothing that a curious neighbor would remember.

He learned that the valley had not let the faith go. Not all of it, not everyone; but more than anyone in the magistrate's office would have believed. The people of Urakami were registered at the temple, like everyone. They stepped on the board at New Year, like everyone. They held Buddhist funerals for their dead. And then they went home and closed their shutters and prayed the prayers their grandparents had taught them, in the old words, and kept the feasts on the days that their calendar told them to keep them, and baptized their babies with water and a few words of Latin that the baptizer had learned from the baptizer before him.

The calendar was kept by an old man named Kichizaemon, in Nakano, the *chōkata*, the keeper of the book. Every year he worked out from it which days were the feasts of the Lord, and which were the days of fasting, and sent word quietly to the households. He kept other books, too, in the bottom of a chest under his winter quilts: a few precious pages copied out by hand, copies of copies, from the books the padres had printed long ago. One of them, which he showed Yoichi only after a full year of visits, was called *Konchirisan no ryaku*. *The Way of Contrition.*

"The padres wrote it for us before they were driven out," the old man said, "because they knew a day might come when there would be no one to hear our confessions. So they left us this, and we have copied it out by hand ever since. It teaches how to be truly sorry for our sins, not from fear of punishment, but from love of God, whom we have wounded. And it says that if a Kirishitan is truly sorry in this way, even if there is no priest, God will forgive him." He turned the brittle pages gently, with the tips of his fingers. "We pray it every year. On the day after the board."

"Every year," said Yoichi.

"Every year." The old man looked at him over the pages. "You think we are cowards."

"No," said Yoichi. "I step too."

"Yes. I know." Kichizaemon closed the book. "Let me tell you why we step, Tomasu. Then you can decide for yourself what you think of us." He was quiet a moment. "There was a man named Bastian, a Japanese, a catechist, who went among the villages of the Sotome coast, north of here, after the priests were gone, and taught the people there. He made the calendar we keep. They say he was caught only a few years ago, and killed in Nagasaki. Before he died, he left words for us. He said that we were to keep the faith for seven generations. And after seven generations, the padres would come back to Japan on a great black ship, and we would be able to confess our sins again every week. And we would sing our songs aloud in the streets, and no one would stop us."

The old man's eyes were bright.

"Seven generations," he said. "I will not see it. My son will not, nor his son. But it will come, and when it comes, there must be someone here to meet the ship. Someone to say, *We kept it. We waited for you.* That is why we step, Tomasu. Not to save our skins. Our skins are not worth so much. We step so that the children will live, and be baptized, and learn the prayers, and teach them to their children. If we all refused, there would be no one left in Urakami by the next New Year. The faith would end here. And Bastian's ship would come into the harbor and find no one waiting."

He laid his hand on the chest with the books in it.

"I don't know if it is right," he said. "I have asked God many times. I think he understands. I think he knows what we carry. But I don't know. Every year I put my foot on that board and something in me breaks a little. And every year I come home and pray the *Konchirisan* and he mends it. That is all I know."

* * *

So Yoichi stepped.

Every New Year, in the first days of the first month, the board came down the lane in Kōya-machi, and he stood in his doorway and waited his turn, and put his foot on the cold copper face of the mother and her dead son, and walked across. And then he went home and shut the door and prayed the *Konchirisan*, which he had copied out for himself by lamplight on cheap paper in Kichizaemon's kitchen, and kept folded inside the lining of his winter coat.

*O Deus, who are infinitely good and worthy to be loved above all things: I am sorry with my whole heart that I have offended you.*

Each year it was a little harder, not easier. He had thought it would grow easier, the way the copper had grown smooth under the feet of the city. It did not.

His grandmother stepped beside him five more times. In the winter of the fourth year of Kanbun, when the sleet came off the harbor again, she took a cough that would not leave her, and she knew before he did what it meant. She lay on her quilt by the brazier with her eyes half-closed and asked him to say the prayers for her, the *Credo* and the *Ave Maria* and the *Pater Noster*, over and over, all through the last night, and he did. Toward morning she opened her eyes and looked at him with great clearness, and said, "Isabel. Isabel will be there." And a little later, very softly, "Marina." As if someone had called her by her name from the next room, and she was answering.

The monks of Daion-ji came and chanted the sutras over her, and she was buried in the temple ground under the name of Toku. Afterward, as Kichizaemon had told him the people of Urakami did for their own dead, Yoichi went home and closed the shutters and prayed the old prayers over her once more, every one he knew, to undo the sutras. *Forgive us our trespasses, as we forgive those who trespass against us.* He prayed them on his knees on the floor where her loom had stood, and then he sat in the empty room with his hands in his lap and the smell of indigo in the lane outside, and understood that Kyūemon had been right. He was alone now. Quite alone. Except that he was not.

He did not marry. There were women in the ward who would have had him, and a go-between who came twice with offers. He sent her away with apologies both times. He told her it was because he was poor, and a turnkey, and no family wanted its daughter to marry a man who carried the dead out of the prison. That was true. It was not the reason. The reason was that he would not take a wife into the dark house he lived in without telling her what it was, and he could not tell her.

He stayed at the prison. He might have left. There was other work in Nagasaki for a man with strong arms. But in the second year after Kyūemon's death more prisoners came through the gate, from Bungo this time, far to the east, roped together just as the Kōri people had been. And in the years after, others, in ones and twos and tens. And Yoichi found that he could not leave them. He carried the water to them in the evening as he always had. When Gonroku was asleep, or looking the other way, he stood a moment longer at the lattice than he needed to. Sometimes he said nothing at all. Sometimes he said, very quietly, *Zezus. Maria.* And watched their faces change in the dark.

He never knew how many of them understood. He never knew how many of them stepped in the end and went home, or died in the magistrate's rooms, or went out to Nishizaka in the morning. He carried out the ones who died. He said the prayers for them in his head as he carried them, the way Kyūemon had taught him without words, by doing it.

And every New Year, he stepped.
