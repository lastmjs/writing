# V. The Brass
## 真鍮 · Kanbun 9 (1669)

By the ninth year of Kanbun, Yoichi was thirty-five, and the oldest turnkey at the Sakuramachi prison. Gonroku had gone home to his brother's village in Isahaya with a bad back and a small purse of silver. The new boy who came to take his place was called Sukezō. He was seventeen, the youngest son of a ward runner in Hamano-machi, all elbows and appetite, and he was afraid of the dead.

He tried to hide it. He made jokes about the prisoners, loud ones, the kind Gonroku used to make. But the first time Yoichi sent him in to carry out a body, a Bungo man who had died in the night of a fever, Sukezō came out white to the lips and went behind the guardroom and was sick.

Yoichi found him there afterward, squatting against the wall with his arms over his head.

"It's all right," Yoichi said, and sat down beside him in the dust. "I was sick too. The first time."

"You?" The boy looked up, disbelieving. "You don't even blink. Gonroku-san said you'd carry a corpse in one arm and your rice bowl in the other."

"Gonroku said a lot of things." Yoichi was quiet a moment. "Listen. When you carry them, don't look away. Look at them. They were people this morning. Somebody's father. It's easier if you remember that. It's harder, but it's easier. Do you understand?"

Sukezō did not understand. But he nodded, and the next time, Yoichi saw him look.

That evening Yoichi showed him how to take the water around. How to hold the ladle low, so that the ones who could not sit up could drink. How to wait while they drank, and not hurry them.

"Why bother?" Sukezō said. "Half of them are going to Nishizaka anyway."

Yoichi held the ladle to the lattice for a woman from Bungo whose hands were too swollen to take it, and waited while she drank.

"So am I," he said. "Someday. So are you. Everybody's going somewhere."

The boy laughed uncertainly, as if it had been a joke. Yoichi let him think so.

* * *

He walked out to Urakami on a rest day in the tenth month, with a basket of dried sardines on his back and the first cold wind of autumn at his heels. He found Kichizaemon sitting on the veranda of his farmhouse in Nakano wrapped in two quilts, though the sun was out. The old man had grown very frail in the last year. His son Matakichi, a broad, silent farmer of forty, was already doing most of the work of the calendar, though he still brought every page to his father to check.

"You've heard," said Kichizaemon, before Yoichi had even set down the basket.

"Heard what?"

"About the new boards." The old man's mouth twisted. "Matakichi heard it at the market. The magistrate has had new ones made. Not of wood. Of brass. Twenty of them. He gave the order to a caster in the city, a man called Hagiwara Yūsa, and they say he has finished them. Kōno-sama is very pleased. The old boards were wearing out, you see. The medals in them were worn down to nothing. You could hardly tell any longer what you were stepping on." He laughed, a dry rattle in his chest. "Too many feet. Now they have new ones, cast in brass, so they will last forever."

Yoichi sat down slowly on the edge of the veranda.

"What is on them?" he said.

"Different pictures, they say. The Mother with the child. The Mother holding him dead, as on the old boards. The Lord with his crown of thorns. And the Lord on the cross." Kichizaemon pulled the quilts closer around him. "Matakichi's wife's cousin is married to a man who sweeps the yard at the magistrate's west office, and he saw them unpacked. He said they are very fine. Better work than anything the padres ever brought from Europe. He said the Lord on the cross looked so real, so sad, that he was afraid to look at it."

For a while none of them said anything. A flock of sparrows came down into the stubble of the paddy below the house and went up again all at once, like a handful of thrown grain.

"Brass," said Kichizaemon at last. "So it will last forever." He shook his head. "Fools. Nothing made of metal lasts forever. Not under feet. Not under the feet of the whole of Kyushu, year after year. They will see. In a hundred years his face will be worn as smooth as a pebble, and they will have to make new ones again." He paused. "It is strange, though. Isn't it? That they will go to such trouble. That they should need, so badly, to have his face under our feet."

He looked at Yoichi.

"You are thinking of not stepping," he said.

Yoichi did not answer. He did not know the answer. He had not known, until the old man said it aloud, that he had been thinking anything at all.

"Listen to me, Tomasu." Kichizaemon put out a thin hand from the quilts and gripped his sleeve. "You know what will happen if you refuse. They will take you to the magistrate's office, and they will ask you who taught you. And they will not stop asking. They have ways of asking that no man can stand. Not you. Not anyone. You have seen what they do."

"I know."

"Then you know what you carry. Not only yourself. Me. Matakichi. His children. Kame and her family. The whole of Nakano, perhaps the whole of Urakami. Everyone you have ever sat with in this house."

"I know," Yoichi said again, very low. "I would never..."

"Every man says *never*. Every man who ever went into the magistrate's rooms." The old man's grip tightened. "I am not saying this to frighten you. I am saying it because I love you, and because I have been doing this for forty years, and I have seen what happens. Step, Tomasu. Step, and come home and pray the *Konchirisan*, and come out here in the spring and help Matakichi with the planting, and keep the faith alive. That is your work. That is all of our work. To keep it alive until the ship comes."

He let go of the sleeve. He was breathing hard.

"Yes," said Yoichi. "Yes. All right."

He believed it when he said it.

* * *

He walked home through the dusk, past the foot of Nishizaka, and this time he stopped and looked up at the bare hill against the darkening sky. There was nothing there now. Grass. A few crows. The bamboo fence was gone. You would never have known.

He thought about Kyūemon, and the water running into his eyes. He thought about Peter, warming himself by the fire in the high priest's courtyard on the night of the arrest, while a serving girl peered at his face. *You were with him. You are one of them.* And Peter saying, *Woman, I do not know him.* And the cock crowing.

Eleven times now, Yoichi had stepped. Eleven New Years. Eleven times *I do not know him.* He had prayed the *Konchirisan* after every one, and each time he had felt the peace come back, the mending Kichizaemon spoke of, like warm water poured into a cracked bowl. He did not doubt that it was real. He did not doubt that he was forgiven. That was not the trouble.

The trouble was that he could no longer remember what it would be like to say anything else.

That night, alone in the two rooms in Kōya-machi, he knelt on the floor where his grandmother's loom had stood and prayed. Not for courage. He was too honest to pray for courage; he did not know whether he wanted it. He prayed the only prayer he had ever been sure of, the first one Kyūemon gave him.

*If you are there, let me know you.*

And then, after a long time: *Whatever it is you want from my feet, give it to me. Either way. Whichever way. Only let it be you.*

* * *

The New Year came on in the usual way. There was frost on the gutters in Kōya-machi and the dye vats steamed in the mornings. The pine boughs went up at the gates. The mallets started in the lanes, pounding rice for the New Year cakes, and the sound of them echoed off the hills all over the city like a great slow heartbeat.

The widow in the rooms next to Yoichi's, a dyer's widow called Osen, had a small daughter, Okiku, who was four years old that year by the old way of counting and very interested in everything. On the last evening of the old year she came and stood in Yoichi's doorway in her padded jacket, sucking her finger, and watched him mend his sandal strap.

"Tell me a story," she said.

"I don't know any stories."

"Everyone knows stories."

He thought about it. Then he set down the sandal.

"Once," he said, "there was a man who kept a hundred sheep."

"What's a sheep?"

"It's like a goat. A white goat, with thick hair like cotton. Anyway, he had a hundred of them. And one night he counted them, and there were only ninety-nine."

Okiku took her finger out of her mouth. "One ran away."

"One ran away. It was a foolish sheep, and it had wandered off into the hills, and gotten itself lost in the dark. So what do you think the man did?"

"Went to sleep," said Okiku, who was practical. "He still had ninety-nine."

"No," said Yoichi. "He left the ninety-nine, and he went out into the hills in the dark, and he looked for the lost one. All night. Over rocks and through thorns. And when he found it, at last, he picked it up and put it on his shoulders, and carried it all the way home. And he was so happy that he called all his neighbors and said, *Come and celebrate with me, I've found my sheep that was lost.*"

Okiku considered this.

"That's a strange man," she said finally.

"Yes," said Yoichi. "He is."

Her mother called her from next door, and she ran off. He sat in the doorway with the sandal in his lap and watched the dark come down over the lane, and the lamps come on one by one behind the paper windows. Somewhere a temple bell began tolling out the old year. He sat and listened to it for a long time.

Three days later, on the morning of the fourth day of the first month of the tenth year of Kanbun, the officials came to Kōya-machi with the brass.
