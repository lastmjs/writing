# VI. The Treading
## 絵踏 · The fourth day of the first month, Kanbun 10 (1670)

It was a bright, hard, cold morning, the sky over the harbor scraped clean by the wind. There was a skin of ice on the dye vats. The ward elder, Shōzaemon, had his table set up outside his gate as always, with the brazier and the writing box and the register open to the first page, its corners weighted with stones against the wind. Shōzaemon was an old man now too, round and kind and nervous, who had known Yoichi since he was a child running in the lane, and had written his grandmother's name in the register every year for thirty years.

Two officials from the magistrate's office stood behind him in their stiff robes, stamping their feet. Between them, on a folding stool, sat a wooden box bound in iron, with the magistrate's crest on the lid.

The whole ward was out in the lane. There was more talk than usual, and more craning of necks. Everyone had heard about the new boards.

When the officials opened the box, a little murmur ran down the line, as it does at a festival when the first float comes around the corner.

The thing they lifted out and laid on the straw mat was not a board at all. It was a plaque of brass, a little longer than a man's hand from wrist to fingertip, with a raised rim all around like the edge of a tray. It caught the low winter sun and shone, gold, like nothing anyone in Kōya-machi had ever seen lying in the dirt of their lane. In the middle of it, standing up out of the metal so that it threw a small shadow, was the figure of a man on a cross.

His arms were stretched wide along the crossbeam, and his head had fallen forward onto his shoulder, and his feet were nailed together, one over the other, at the foot of the cross. A cloth was knotted around his hips, its loose end lifting as if in a wind. Above his head was a little board with letters on it in the foreign script. And below him, very small and fine, there was a city with walls and towers and domed roofs, a city in a far country, outside whose walls they had killed him.

Yoichi was halfway down the line, between the dyer's family and Osen and the child. He could see it from where he stood. The sun was full on it.

He could see the face.

It was not like the copper on the old board, worn to a shape. It was new. It was sharp. Every line of it was as clear as the day it came out of Hagiwara Yūsa's mold. The closed eyes. The thin cheeks. The mouth a little open, as if at the very end he had been about to say something, and had not had the breath.

Kichizaemon's cousin's husband had been right. He looked so real, and so sad, that you were afraid to look at him.

The line began to move.

* * *

The first family stepped. A woman laughed nervously, in the way people do at funerals. A child cried. One of the officials said something sharp, and the laughing stopped. The dyer's family went up, all six of them, the old dyer leaning on his son, the youngest girl hopping across on one foot as if it were a game. Their bare feet came down one after another, on the cross, on the outstretched arms, on the face.

Yoichi's heart was beating very slowly and very hard, like the mallets in the lane.

*Step, Tomasu.* Kichizaemon's voice. *Step, and come home and pray. That is your work.* He knew it was true. He had promised. He had stepped eleven times, he could step twelve. It was cold brass, that was all. The same as the copper, only newer. He would put his foot on it and walk across and bow to Shōzaemon, and go home and close the door and say the *Konchirisan*, and in the spring he would go out to Urakami and help Matakichi plant the rice. Everyone would be safe. That was the right thing. He knew it was the right thing.

Then it was Osen's turn, and Okiku's.

Osen stepped quickly, with her eyes on the ground, as most of them did, and turned at once to lift the child. But Okiku had seen the brass shining in the sun and wanted to look at it. She wriggled down out of her mother's arms and squatted beside the mat in her padded jacket and peered at the little brass man, very close, the way she had stood in Yoichi's doorway and peered at his sandal strap.

"Who is he?" she said, loudly, in her clear high child's voice.

The lane went quiet. One of the officials frowned.

"A bad foreign god," Osen whispered, scarlet, grabbing at her. "Hurry up. Step on him."

Okiku looked at the face for a moment longer.

"He's sad," she said.

And then, because her mother was pulling at her arm, and because she was four years old and did not understand anything, she stood up and put her small bare foot on the face of Christ, carefully, the way a child steps on a stone in a stream, and hopped off, and ran to the table to watch Shōzaemon write her name.

Yoichi stood looking at the brass.

He would never be able to say, afterward, what happened in him then. There was nothing to see. He did not hear a voice. He did not decide anything, any more than a man decides to catch his breath when he comes up out of deep water. He only knew, standing there in the lane with the wind in his face, that the old man had been right, that it was not a thing that was done with words or with reasons. It was done with feet. And that his feet were not going to move.

It was not courage. He was more afraid than he had ever been in his life. It was not anger at those who stepped, at Osen or the dyer or the child or Kichizaemon or his own grandmother; he had never felt so tender toward any of them. It was not even, exactly, faith, if faith was something a man held in his head.

It was only this: that he had seen a face. A tired face, a man's face, with its mouth a little open as if he had been about to say something. And he knew whose face it was, and he loved it, and he could not.

*He has already been stepped on. For me.*

*Peter, do you love me?*

"Yoichi." It was Shōzaemon, looking up from the register with his brush in his hand. "Yoichi. It's your turn."

Yoichi walked forward to the edge of the mat and stopped.

The lane was very quiet. He could hear the wind snapping the paper of the New Year decorations on the gateposts, and somewhere a long way off, the mallets still going in another ward, pounding rice. He could feel everyone looking at him. He could feel the officials straighten.

"Yoichi," said Shōzaemon again. His voice had changed. "Go on."

Yoichi looked down at the brass. The sun was on it. The little city with its towers. The arms spread out wide, as if to take in the whole of Kōya-machi, the whole of Nagasaki, every foot that had ever come down on him. There were faint marks on the face already, from the dyer's family and from Osen and from Okiku's small foot. Dust. A smear of mud. The face did not mind them. It was not looking at them. Its eyes were closed.

"I can't," Yoichi said.

He had not known he was going to say it out loud. His voice sounded very strange to him, quite calm and ordinary, as if he were telling Shōzaemon he could not come to dinner.

The ward elder stood up from behind his table. He was trembling. He came around the side of the table, very quickly for so heavy a man, and took Yoichi by the arm and put his mouth close to his ear.

"Are you sick?" he whispered. "Are you drunk? For the love of all the Buddhas, Yoichi, think what you're doing. Just put your foot on it. Your heel. Anywhere. Touch the edge, and I'll write that you stepped. Nobody will look. I'll swear to it. Do it now, quickly, before..."

Yoichi turned and looked at him. The old man's eyes were wet. He had known him all his life.

"Thank you, Shōzaemon-sama," Yoichi said, very low. "But they would know you lied. And then they would hang you next to me."

"Yoichi..."

"I'm sorry," said Yoichi. "I'm sorry for the trouble. Truly I am."

He knelt down on the frozen ground at the edge of the straw mat, in front of the whole ward. He put his hands together. And then he bent forward, slowly, the way you bow before an altar, or before your father, and laid his forehead on the cold brass at the foot of the cross, beside the nailed feet.

He heard Osen cry out behind him. He heard the dyer's wife say *Oh, no, no.* He heard the iron-bound box clatter off its stool as one of the officials shoved past it. He heard nothing else, for a moment, but his own breathing, and the wind, and the beating of his heart. The brass was very cold against his forehead. It smelled of metal and dust.

*My Lord*, he thought, *and my God.*

Then hands took hold of his shoulders and dragged him up.
