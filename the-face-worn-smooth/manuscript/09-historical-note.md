# A Historical Note
## 史実

Yoichi, his grandmother, Kyūemon, Kichizaemon, Sukezō, Mitsu, and the people of Kōya-machi are invented. The world they live in is not.

**The Christian century.** Jesuit missionaries reached Japan in 1549. Within two generations there were perhaps 300,000 Japanese Christians, and Nagasaki, a fishing village given to the Jesuits by the Christian lord Ōmura Sumitada, had become an almost entirely Christian port city with churches, hospitals, and confraternities. The churches named in the story stood there: Todos os Santos, on the hill where the temple of Shuntoku-ji was later built, and Santo Domingo, whose site became the residence of the city's deputy governor (*daikan*). The *Dochirina Kirishitan*, a catechism in question-and-answer form, was printed in Japanese by the Jesuit press in Nagasaki. The form of the Lord's Prayer that Yoichi's grandmother whispers follows its 1600 text.

**The ban.** Toyotomi Hideyoshi had twenty-six Christians crucified on the hill of Nishizaka in Nagasaki in 1597. The Tokugawa shogunate banned Christianity throughout Japan in 1614. Churches were demolished, missionaries expelled, and believers pressed to renounce their faith. Every household had to register at a Buddhist temple, which certified that its members were not Christian. Under the Nagasaki magistrate Takenaka Shigeyoshi (in office 1629 to 1631), Christians were taken to the volcanic hot springs of Unzen and scalded with boiling water to make them apostatize. The pit (*ana-tsurushi*) was used from the early 1630s. The victim was bound tightly, hung upside down in a pit of filth, and given a small cut at the temple to slow death, with one hand left free to signal apostasy. Some lasted more than a week. The Jesuit provincial Cristóvão Ferreira renounced his faith in the pit in 1633. The Japanese Jesuit Julian Nakaura, who as a boy had travelled to Rome and met the Pope, died in it the same year. The last priest known to be at liberty in Japan, Konishi Mancio, was martyred in 1644. After that, Japanese Christians had no clergy for more than two hundred years.

**E-fumi and the fumi-e.** The ritual of *e-fumi*, "picture-treading," began in Nagasaki in 1629. Residents were made to step on a sacred image, and anyone who refused was arrested as a Christian. The first images were on paper. Later they were wooden boards set with devotional medals confiscated from Christians. In 1669 the Nagasaki magistrate Kōno Gon'emon Michisada had the caster Hagiwara Yūsa make twenty brass plaques, because the older images were wearing out. One tradition holds that only nineteen survive and that the twentieth fell into the sea on its way to Amakusa. By the late seventeenth century e-fumi was held in Nagasaki every year at New Year, ward by ward, with every resident taking part, children included. It also spread to most domains of Kyushu. Nagasaki abandoned the practice in 1858, after the treaties with the Western powers. The brass plaque on the cover of this book belonged to the Nagasaki magistrate's office and is now in the Tokyo National Museum. Its face has been worn completely smooth.

**The Kōri collapse.** In 1657, a chance remark by a farmer named Hyōsaku from Kōri village in the domain of Ōmura led the Nagasaki magistrate Kurokawa Masanao to uncover a large community of hidden Christians around the village. This was the *Kōri kuzure*, the "Kōri collapse." Six hundred and eight people were arrested, including small children. Seventy-eight died in custody, ninety-nine were released, twenty were imprisoned for life, and four hundred and eleven were sentenced to death. One hundred and thirty-one were beheaded at Hōkobaru in Ōmura in 1658. The rest were divided among other domains for execution: one hundred and twenty-three in Nagasaki, sixty-four in Hirado, fifty-six in Shimabara, and thirty-seven in Saga. Further mass arrests followed in Bungo (from 1660) and in the Nōbi region (from 1661). Kyūemon is imagined as one of the Kōri prisoners held in Nagasaki. The Sakuramachi prison, where the story places him, was the city's prison in this period.

**The hidden Christians.** Not everyone gave up the faith. In the villages around Nagasaki, above all in the Urakami valley just north of the city, on the Sotome coast, and later in the Gotō Islands, communities of *senpuku Kirishitan* ("Christians in hiding") kept their faith in secret for two hundred and fifty years. Outwardly they were Buddhist, and they stepped on the fumi-e when ordered. In secret they baptized their children through a lay baptizer (*mizukata*), kept the Church's feasts by a calendar held by a lay leader (*chōkata*), and handed down prayers (*orasho*) by memory, many of them in garbled Latin and Portuguese. After Buddhist funerals they said prayers to "undo" the sutras. They relied especially on *Konchirisan no ryaku* ("A Summary of Contrition"), a short guide to perfect contrition printed by the Jesuits in 1603. It taught that a Christian who could not reach a priest could still receive God's forgiveness through sorrow for sin that came from love of God. In the story, Yoichi and the people of Urakami pray from it after treading on the image, as hidden communities are recorded to have done.

Their liturgical calendar was traditionally credited to a Japanese catechist called Bastian, who worked on the Sotome coast. His prophecy was remembered there for centuries: after seven generations, confessors would come again in a great black ship, Christians would be able to confess every week, and they would sing their hymns aloud in the street.

**The discovery.** On 17 March 1865, a few weeks after the new Ōura church was consecrated for the foreign community in Nagasaki, about fifteen people from Urakami came to it and approached the French missionary Bernard Petitjean as he prayed. A woman named Sugimoto Yuri, whose baptismal name was Isabelina, told him, "The heart of all of us here is the same as your heart," and asked where the image of Santa Maria was. The visitors told him that they kept Christmas and were then in the season of sorrow, which was Lent. The story's last chapter follows Petitjean's own account of that afternoon. Mitsu, who stands beside Yuri, is fictional. In the years that followed, tens of thousands of hidden Christians came forward across the region.

The persecution was not quite over. In 1867 the authorities began arresting the Christians of Urakami, and from 1870 more than three thousand of them were sent into exile in distant domains, where some six hundred died. This was the "fourth Urakami collapse." Under Western diplomatic pressure, the edict boards against Christianity were taken down in 1873, and the exiles came home. The returning Christians of Urakami later built a great brick church with their own hands on the site of the village headman's house, where they had once been made to step on the fumi-e. That church stood about five hundred meters from the hypocenter of the atomic bomb dropped on Nagasaki on 9 August 1945, and was destroyed. It has since been rebuilt.

**Who stepped, and who did not.** The real history of the hidden Christians is not mainly a story of people who refused to step. Most of them stepped, year after year, for two and a half centuries, so that the faith would survive for their children. They grieved over it and asked God's forgiveness. Without them there would have been no one left to walk into the Ōura church in 1865. Thousands of others, from the 1590s to the 1870s, refused and died. The Catholic Church has beatified or canonized several hundred of them by name, and the rest are known only to God. This story tries to honor both. Kyūemon's mercy toward the mother who steps, Kichizaemon's counsel, Yoichi's tenderness toward his neighbors, and the line Mitsu's family writes on their prayer ("*For Tomasu of the prison, who did not step. And for those of us who did.*") are meant to say that the fumi-e did not divide the faithful from the faithless. On both sides of it there were people who loved the face beneath their feet.

**Further reading.** Shūsaku Endō's novel *Silence* (1966) is the best-known work of fiction about the fumi-e and the apostasy of priests under persecution. Nagayo Yoshirō's novella *Seidō no Kirisuto* (*The Bronze Christ*, 1923) imagines the caster of the brass fumi-e. This story is indebted to both and follows neither. For the history, see Stephen Turnbull, *The Kakure Kirishitan of Japan* (1998), and C. R. Boxer, *The Christian Century in Japan, 1549–1650* (1951). The UNESCO World Heritage nomination "Hidden Christian Sites in the Nagasaki Region" (inscribed 2018) and the materials of Japan's Agency for Cultural Affairs are also useful.
