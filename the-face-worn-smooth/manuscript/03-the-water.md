# III. The Water
## 水 · Meireki 4 (1658), spring into summer

He asked him.

It was the first warm night of the year, in the third month, when the plum blossom was over and the cherries had not yet come, and the air in the passage smelled of wet earth instead of frost. The prisoners from Kōri were fewer by then. Some had died. Some had stepped and gone home. Among those who went home was the boy who had looked at the sea. His mother had stepped for him, and then she had stepped herself, with her face like stone, and Yoichi had seen Kyūemon reach through the lattice as they passed, and touch her sleeve, and say something to her that made her stop and cover her mouth with her hand. Afterward Yoichi asked him what he had said.

"I told her that God sees the heart," the old man said. "And that she must go home and teach the boy his prayers."

"You don't despise her?"

Kyūemon looked at him as if the question made no sense.

"She is my sister," he said. "She is carrying more than I am. Why would I despise her?"

So that night, when the passage was dark and Gonroku was asleep, Yoichi crouched by the lattice with the bucket beside him and asked the question his grandmother had sent him to ask. How do you know? How can you be sure? You have never seen him. No one alive has seen him. You have only the word of foreign priests who were all killed or driven out before I was born.

Kyūemon listened to all of it without moving. When Yoichi had finished, the old man was quiet for a while.

"There was one of his disciples," he said at last, "who was like you. His name was Tomas. After the Lord rose from the dead, the others told him, *We have seen him*, and Tomas said, *Unless I see the marks of the nails in his hands, and put my finger in them, and put my hand into his side, I will not believe.*" Kyūemon smiled in the dark. "A farmer's answer. Show me the field. Let me put my hands in the dirt."

"And?"

"And eight days later, the Lord came to them again, through the locked door, and he said to Tomas, *Put your finger here, and see my hands. Put out your hand and place it in my side. Do not disbelieve, but believe.* And Tomas fell down on his knees and said, *My Lord and my God.*" The old man's voice had grown very soft. "Then the Lord said, *You believe because you have seen me. Blessed are those who have not seen, and yet believe.*"

"That is a story," Yoichi said.

"Yes. And I am not the Lord, and I cannot show you my hands, except that they are like this." Kyūemon held them up to the lattice in the faint light of the lamp. Two of the fingers on the left had been broken at the magistrate's office and had set crooked. "But I will tell you how I know. I have spoken to him every day of my life for sixty years. In the field and in the dark and in the cave in the hills where we went to pray, and in here. He does not answer with a voice. But he answers. Sometimes it is only peace, where there should be none. Sometimes it is a strength that is not mine. I am an old man and a coward by nature, turnkey. You have no idea what a coward I am. And yet you have watched me. You tell me: where does it come from?"

Yoichi had no answer. He thought of the old man being carried in from the magistrate's office with his feet wrapped in rags, and the way he had said *thank you* for a ladle of water.

"I don't know," he said.

"No. Neither did I, once. And then one day I did." Kyūemon lowered his hands. "Your grandmother. She was baptized?"

Yoichi stiffened. He had said nothing of his grandmother. He had been careful. And then he understood that he had not needed to say anything; that he had been asking the old man's questions with her words for weeks, and Kyūemon had heard her in them as clearly as a bell.

"Yes," he said.

"Then she has given you the faith already. All I can tell you is that it is true." Kyūemon reached through the lattice and laid his crooked fingers on Yoichi's wrist, very lightly. "Don't ask me to make you sure. Nobody can make you sure. Only ask him. Tonight, when you lie down. Say, *If you are there, let me know you.* That is all. It is a prayer he has never once refused."

* * *

Yoichi did not know, afterward, when it had happened. There was no voice. There was no light. There was only a night in the fourth month, and then many nights, when he lay on his mat in the guardroom and said the old man's prayer into the dark, and then the prayer his grandmother had taught him, *Ten ni mashimasu warera ga on-oya*, Our Father, and listened. And there was a morning when he woke before dawn and lay listening to the sea birds and the temple bells beginning across the city, and found that something in him that had been clenched all his life had opened, like a hand. As if a person he had been waiting for in a dark house had come in quietly and sat down beside him, and he had not heard the door.

He told his grandmother. She held his face in her hands for a long time and said nothing.

He told Kyūemon, at the lattice. And then he said the other thing, the one he had been carrying for days without knowing how to set it down.

"I want to be baptized."

The old man shut his eyes.

"Do you know what you are asking?" he said. "You have seen what is done to us. You have carried the bodies out. You have heard the sounds from the magistrate's office."

"Yes."

"If they find you, it will be done to you. And to your grandmother."

"Yes."

"And there are no priests anymore. Not one, in all Japan. There is no one to hear your confession, or give you the Lord's body at the Mass. You will be alone, Yoichi. Alone in a city that hates what you are, for the rest of your life. Do you understand that?"

"I am alone now," Yoichi said. "I have been alone all my life. I didn't know it until you."

Kyūemon opened his eyes and looked at him for a long time.

"Then wait," he said. "Wait until the time is right. I will know when."

* * *

The sentences came down from the magistrate's office at the end of the sixth month. One hundred and twenty-three of the Kōri people were to die in Nagasaki. The rest were sent to Ōmura and Hirado and Shimabara and Saga, to die there, so that the lesson would be taught in every domain. Kyūemon's name was on the Nagasaki list.

They would go out to the hill of Nishizaka in the morning. The night before, Yoichi brought the water as he always did, and went along the lattice, and came last to the old man, who was kneeling up in the straw, waiting for him.

"Now," Kyūemon said quietly. "It is time."

Yoichi glanced back along the passage. Gonroku was at the far end, by the brazier, his back turned, eating his rice. Up and down the cell, the Kōri people had fallen silent. They had understood before he had. Some of them had shifted in the straw, close together, so that their bodies made a screen.

"Kneel down," said Kyūemon. "Here. Put your head against the bars."

Yoichi knelt. The timber was rough and cool against his forehead. Through the gap between the bars he could see the old man's knees, his hands, the ladle he had taken from the bucket.

"What name will you take?" Kyūemon whispered.

He had not thought of it. And then he had, without thinking.

"Tomas," he said. "The one who wanted to see."

He felt the old man's hand rest on his head. It was trembling. Then the water came, cold, through his hair and over his forehead and into his eyes, and the old man's voice above him said the words in the strange old tongue of the padres, softly and very carefully, as if each word were a coin he had kept hidden for thirty years and was now spending all at once:

"*Tomasu. Ego te baputizo, in nomine Pateru, e Hīrio, e Supiritsu Santo. Amen.*"

Behind the old man, very quietly, the people of Kōri said *Amen.*

Yoichi stayed with his head against the bars, the water running down his face, and did not know whether he was weeping. He felt the old man's hand on his head still, steadying itself, and then, through the bars, the dry brush of a kiss on his hair.

"Listen to me, Tomasu," Kyūemon whispered. "There is something I must tell you, and there is no more time. One day the question will come to you too. Perhaps on a board on the ground. Perhaps some other way. I don't know. Don't be afraid of it. And listen. If your feet move—" his voice caught, and steadied— "if your feet move, he is not finished with you. Do you hear me? Peter denied him three times, on the night he was taken, and swore he had never known the man. And afterward the Lord came and found him by the sea, and made him breakfast, and asked him three times, *Do you love me?* And Peter said yes. Three times. One yes for every no. And at the end of his life Peter was crucified for him, upside down, in the city of Rome. So. Whatever happens. He is not finished with you."

"And if my feet do not move?"

The old man's hand pressed his hair, once.

"Then I will be waiting for you," he said. "Now go. Before your friend turns around."

* * *

In the morning, Yoichi walked beside the line of prisoners to Nishizaka, because it was his work. The road ran north out of the city along the edge of the harbor and climbed the bare hill above the water. The officials had put up a fence of bamboo around the top. A crowd had gathered outside the fence to watch, as crowds always did. The sun was very bright on the bay. Out on the water the Dutch ship lay at anchor off Dejima, and a little fleet of fishing boats was coming in with the morning's catch.

He had seen executions before. He had never seen one like this.

The people of Kōri knelt down in rows on the hill. Some of them were singing. Not loudly; they had sung in whispers for so long that perhaps they had forgotten how to do it any other way. But they were singing. Kyūemon was in the second row. He did not look for Yoichi in the crowd of guards, and Yoichi was glad, because he did not know what his face would have done.

The old man's lips were moving. Yoichi could not hear him, but he did not need to. He knew the words. *Ten ni mashimasu warera ga on-oya.* And then, at the end, the two names that every Christian said at the last, if they could say anything at all.

*Zezus. Maria.*

The sword came down.

Afterward, when it was over, the bodies were burned on the hill, and the ashes were taken out in a boat and scattered on the sea beyond the harbor mouth, so that no one could gather them up and keep them, as the Christians had once done with the bones of their dead. Yoichi watched the smoke go up from Nishizaka all afternoon, gray against the summer sky, and drift out over the water.

That night he did not go back to his mat in the guardroom. He went home to Kōya-machi through the dark streets, and slid open the door of his grandmother's room, and knelt down in front of her without a word. She looked at his face and knew. She took his head into her lap as she had when he was four years old, and they stayed like that until the morning, praying for the dead, which was a thing she had told him Christians did, though she could not remember any longer exactly how.
