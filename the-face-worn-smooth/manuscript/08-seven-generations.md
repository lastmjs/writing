# VIII. Seven Generations
## 七代 · Genji 2 (1865)

The church stood on the hillside above the foreigners' settlement at Ōura, new and white, with a tower and pointed windows of colored glass and a cross on the top that could be seen from the harbor. The people of Nagasaki called it the French Temple. They went in crowds to stare at it, as they went to stare at the steamships and the foreigners' houses with their glass windows and their iron balconies. The French priest who kept it let them in and showed them around, smiling, and said little. He was not permitted to preach to Japanese. The edicts were still posted on the notice boards at every crossroads, as they had been for two hundred and fifty years: *The evil sect of the Kirishitan is strictly forbidden.*

On a cool, bright afternoon at the beginning of spring, in the second month of the second year of Genji, the seventeenth of March by the foreigners' calendar, a group of men and women from Urakami came over the hill and stood outside the gate.

There were fifteen of them, perhaps. Farmers. You could tell by their hands, and by the way they stood. They looked like any other party of country people come to gawk at the French Temple. They had come in ones and twos, by different paths, and met at the gate as if by chance.

Among them was a woman of fifty named Mitsu, from Nakano. She had a thin, careful face and a farmer's thick wrists, and she was so frightened she could feel her knees shaking under her kimono.

The priest was working in the garden. He saw them at the gate, and came and unlocked it, and let them in, and led them up the steps into the church, as he had done for so many others.

It was dim inside, after the bright day. It smelled of new wood and candle wax and something else, something sweet, that Mitsu did not know. The windows glowed. At the far end there was an altar with candles on it, and above the altar, a cross.

The priest walked up to the altar and knelt down before it, with his back to them, and bowed his head, and prayed. They stood in a little cluster at the back and watched him. No one spoke. Mitsu found that she was gripping the sleeve of the woman next to her, a woman from another hamlet of the valley, Yuri, whose baptismal name was Isabelina. Yuri's arm was trembling too.

Then Yuri let go of her, and walked up the aisle, and knelt down beside the foreign priest. Mitsu and another woman followed her. Mitsu did not decide to. Her feet simply moved.

Yuri put her hand on her heart. Her voice, when it came, was very low.

"The heart of all of us here," she said, "is the same as your heart."

The priest turned and looked at her. Mitsu saw his face. He was young, with a brown beard, and his face had gone completely still, the way a man's face goes still when he hears a sound he has been listening for all his life and is afraid he imagined.

"Truly?" he said, in his strange, careful Japanese. "Where do you come from?"

"From Urakami," said Yuri. "In Urakami, almost everyone has the same heart as ours." And then, all at once, in a rush, like a child: "Santa Maria. Where is the image of Santa Maria?"

The priest stood up. He was shaking. He led them to the side of the church, where there was a statue on a pedestal, painted, of a young woman in a blue mantle, holding a child.

The women knelt in front of it.

"Yes," Yuri said. "Yes, it is Santa Maria. Look at the honored child Zezus, in her arms."

Mitsu knelt beside her and looked up at the face of the mother and the face of the child. She had never seen them before, not in all her life. She had only ever heard them described. She found that she was weeping, and could not stop, and did not try.

Later, the others told the priest about their calendar, and the feasts. They told him that they kept the feast of the Lord's birth on the twenty-fifth day of the month of frost. They told him that they were keeping now the season of sorrow before the Lord's death, and asked him whether he was keeping it too. When he said that he was, that this was the season the padres called Lent, they looked at each other and smiled, and could not stop smiling. They had kept the calendar for seven generations, and it had not lost count.

Mitsu said nothing. She knelt in front of Santa Maria with her hands pressed together, and under them, inside her kimono against her skin, folded small and wrapped in oiled paper, she felt the edges of the thing she had brought.

* * *

It was not the paper Sukezō had found in the lining of a dead man's coat. That had crumbled to nothing long ago. It was a copy of a copy of a copy, written out again by each generation of her family when the one before began to fall apart. But it was the same words. Her grandfather had made her learn them by heart when she was a girl, as he had learned them from his father. *O Deus, who are infinitely good, and worthy to be loved above all things.*

And on the outside of the folded paper, in her own hand, as in her mother's and her grandfather's before her, there was a line that was not part of the prayer.

*For Tomasu of the prison, who did not step. And for those of us who did.*

That was all. Her family did not know much more than that about him. They knew that the first of them, the one who had found the paper, had been a turnkey at the old prison in Sakuramachi, and had come out to Urakami a few years after the matter of the brass, and had asked to be baptized, and had married a girl of Nakano and stayed. How he had found his way there, the family did not say. Perhaps it was not known. They knew that on the fourth day of the first month every year, the day the officials used to come with the brass, after they had stepped and come home and closed the shutters, they said the prayers for Tomasu, along with the prayers for their own dead. That was all. It had been enough.

She thought of the brass.

They did not bring it out anymore. They had stopped seven years ago, after the foreign ships came and the treaties were signed. In the last years, before the end, she had heard people say that the faces were all worn away. That you could not see them anymore. That under the feet of nearly two hundred years, the brass had been rubbed as smooth as a pebble in a stream, just as old Kichizaemon in the story had said it would be, and where the face of the Lord had been there was nothing left but smooth, bright metal in the shape of a head, with no features at all.

She had stepped on it herself, every year of her life, from the time her mother first set her bare foot on it as a baby. She had not looked. None of them looked. She knew, now, kneeling on the floor of the French Temple in front of Santa Maria, what she would see if she ever did.

She would see that it was still a face. That it had never been anything else. That they had walked across it, all of them, the whole of Kyushu, for nearly two hundred years, the proud and the fearful, the faithful and the faithless, those who stepped and those who would not, those who believed and those who only did as they were told, and those who stepped with their hearts breaking and went home and prayed the *Konchirisan*. And under all of them, under every foot, he had lain there with his arms spread wide. He had never once pulled his face away. He had let them wear it down to nothing. He had let them do everything to him.

*He was stepped on first. For me.*

Mitsu bowed her head, there in the dim church, before the mother and the child.

* * *

There would be one more persecution. Two years later the officials would come to Urakami in the night, and the arrests would begin again, and before it was over more than three thousand of the people of the valley would be sent away into exile in distant domains, and more than six hundred of them would die there. Mitsu would be one of those sent away. She would come home, and many would not.

But the edicts would come down from the notice boards at last, in the sixth year of Meiji. And the people of Urakami would build a church of their own on the hill above their valley, out of red brick, with their own hands, on the very ground where the village headman's office had once stood, where their parents and grandparents had been made to step on the brass every New Year. And there, as Bastian had promised, they would go to confession every week, and sing their songs aloud in the street, and no one would stop them.

But that was later. Now, on this afternoon at the beginning of spring, Mitsu knelt on the wooden floor in front of Santa Maria with her hands pressed against her heart, over the folded paper, and wept, and could not stop, and did not try. Someone had come to meet them. Someone had been waiting.

The ship had come.

They had kept it.
