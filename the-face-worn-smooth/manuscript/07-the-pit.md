# VII. The Pit
## 穴 · The first month, Kanbun 10 (1670)

They put him in the east cells at Sakuramachi, in the long cell with the lattice of square-cut timber, where he had carried water to Kyūemon twelve years before. Sukezō brought the bucket that evening. He stood outside the lattice with the ladle in his hand and would not look at Yoichi, and his face was blotched and swollen as if he had been crying, or trying not to.

"Why?" he said, at last, in a whisper. "Why? You never said. You never said anything."

Yoichi took the ladle through the bars and drank. The water was very cold. When he had finished, he gave it back, and bowed his head over it a little.

"Thank you," he said. "A blessing on the one who gave it."

The boy stared at him. Then he went away down the passage very fast, and did not come back that night.

* * *

The magistrate's examiners were patient men. They had been doing this work for a long time, and they had learned that a man who would not step was a door, and the important thing was not the man but the room behind the door. They did not ask Yoichi, at first, to step. They asked him who had taught him.

He told them the truth. He had decided, kneeling in the straw the first night, that he would tell them nothing that was not true, and nothing that could harm a living soul.

"An old man from Kōri," he said. "A cultivator. His name was Kyūemon. He was in the east cells at Sakuramachi in the third and fourth years of Meireki. I carried his water. He went to Nishizaka with the others in the seventh month."

The examiners looked at each other. One of them sent a clerk for the old registers. The clerk came back with them and found the name, and the date, and the word *executed*, in a neat hand, and showed it to the senior examiner, who grunted.

"Who else?"

"My grandmother. Toku of Kōya-machi. She was baptized as a child, before the edicts. She had fallen. She remembered the prayers. She taught them to me."

"Where is she?"

"Buried at Daion-ji. Six years ago."

"Who else?"

Yoichi said nothing.

"Who else? A turnkey does not become a Christian in a prison cell, from an old farmer, in a few months. A turnkey does not learn the prayers from an old woman who forgot them thirty years before. Somebody has been teaching you since. Somebody has been giving you books. Who? Where do you go, on your rest days?"

Yoichi was silent.

"Your neighbors say you go out of the city. North. Where?"

He said nothing.

They asked him again the next day. And the next. The asking grew harder each time. On the third day they began to use the water. The fourth day he did not remember afterward, except as a long darkness in which someone kept asking him the same question over and over, very calmly, *Who? Where?*, and he kept answering in his mind, *Ten ni mashimasu warera ga on-oya*, Our Father, who art in heaven, over and over, because it was the only thing he could hold onto, and the words came between him and the question like a wall.

He did not give them a name. He did not know, afterward, how he had not. He had told Kichizaemon *never*, and the old man had been right: every man said *never*. He had not been brave. He had wept, and cried out, and once he had heard himself begging them to stop, in a voice that was not his own. But each time the question came, he found there was nothing in his mouth to answer it with. Only *Our Father*. Only *Zezus, Maria*. As if someone had taken the names out of him and kept them safe, somewhere he could not reach.

After ten days, the senior examiner sat down across from him on the floor of the room at the magistrate's office, and looked at him for a long time, with something that was almost pity.

"You're a fool," he said. "You know that. You've done nothing. You have no priest to protect. Your teachers are dead. No one will ever know what you did. If you step, now, today, you can go home. You can have your post back, even. I'll see to it."

Yoichi looked at him through one eye. The other was swollen shut.

"Thank you," he said. "I'm sorry. I can't."

The examiner sighed and stood up.

"Then you'll go into the pit," he said.

* * *

They took him up to Nishizaka in a cart, on a morning of thin cold rain, along the road by the harbor where he had walked beside Kyūemon. He could not walk himself anymore. He lay on his back in the cart and watched the gray sky go by overhead, and the gulls turning in it, and thought how strange it was that he had walked this road so many times and never once seen the sky above it.

The pit was at the top of the hill. It was a hole in the ground, deep as a well and narrow as a man's shoulders, and in the bottom of it, they told him, was filth. Over the top they had built a frame of timber, with two boards that closed together like a pair of hands, with a hole between them.

They bound his body tightly with rope, from his chest to his knees, so that the blood would not all rush to his head at once, and he would not die too soon. They made a small cut at his temple, behind the ear, so the blood would drip out slowly from there. Then they tied his feet together and hung him by them from the frame, head down into the dark, and closed the boards around his waist, so that his legs were up in the cold rain and air, and the rest of him was below, in the pit.

They left his right hand free. That was the custom. If he wished to give up his faith, he had only to reach up out of the dark and make a sign, and they would pull him out, and give him water, and send for a doctor.

It is not possible to describe the pit. Those few who came out of it alive seldom spoke of it. The darkness and the stench and the pressure in the head, as if the skull were filling up with sand. The sound of your own blood, dripping, slowly, a drop at a time, on to the filth below. The way time comes apart, so that you cannot tell whether you have been there an hour or a day. The voices that are not there. The thirst.

He prayed. When he could not pray, he said the names. When he could not say the names, he thought them. *Zezus. Maria.* When he could not think them, he hung in the dark, and that was all, and someone else held them for him.

Once, in a clear moment, he remembered Peter, who had denied his Lord three times by a fire in a courtyard, and had been crucified at the end, they said, upside down. *One yes for every no.* He had said no eleven times. He thought that if he had laughed, it would have hurt very much. So he only said, inside himself, *Yes*, and then again, *yes*, and kept on, slowly, counting, until he lost count.

Sometimes he thought he saw Kyūemon. Sometimes it was his grandmother, sitting at her loom with the shuttle in her hand, looking at him over her shoulder. Once, very clearly, it was Okiku, squatting at the edge of the straw mat in her padded jacket and looking at the little brass man with her finger in her mouth. *He's sad.* He wanted to tell her: *No, not anymore. Not now.* But she had gone.

He prayed for her. For Osen. For the dyer's family, every one, by name. For Shōzaemon, who had wept, and offered to lie for him. For Kichizaemon and Matakichi and old Kame and the whole valley of Urakami, without saying its name even in his head, in case the walls of the pit could hear. For Gonroku and his bad back. For the examiners, and the men who had used the water, and the men who had bound him with the ropes and closed the boards. *As we forgive those who trespass against us.* His grandmother had said that was the line it would take his whole life to learn. He thought perhaps he had very nearly learned it.

He did not raise his hand.

* * *

On the third night, it was Sukezō who had the watch.

Yoichi knew it was the boy because of the cough, a nervous young man's cough in the cold, and because of the way he paced. Back and forth by the frame. Back and forth. Late, when the other guard had gone down the hill to sleep in the hut, the pacing stopped, and the boy crouched down by the boards, close, and spoke into the gap.

"Yoichi-san." A whisper. "Yoichi-san. Are you alive?"

It took a long time to find a voice. "Yes."

"Raise your hand." The boy's voice was shaking. "Please. Please. They told me. Just raise your hand, and I'll call them, and they'll pull you up. I'll run. I'll go right now. Please."

"No."

"Why? Why?" The boy was weeping now, openly. "I don't understand. You never even told anybody. Nobody knew. You could have just stepped and nobody would ever have known. Why?"

Yoichi hung in the dark and thought about it. It seemed very important to get the answer right. It was perhaps the most important thing he would ever say, and he had very little breath left to say it.

"He was stepped on first," he said at last. "For me. And for you, Sukezō. For you too."

The boy said nothing. Yoichi could hear him breathing.

"Sukezō."

"Yes."

"Water."

There was a long silence. Then a sound of movement, and of the boy's feet going away across the hilltop, and coming back. Something touched Yoichi's hand, his free hand, hanging down in the dark beside his face. A cloth. Soaked in cold rainwater from the barrel by the hut. The boy had lowered it on a cord between the boards. Yoichi's fingers closed on it. He could just get it to his mouth.

It was the best thing he had ever tasted in his life.

"Thank you," he said, when he could. "A blessing on the one who gave it."

He heard the boy begin to cry again, quietly.

"Listen, Sukezō. Tonight. When you lie down. Say this. *If you are there, let me know you.* That's all. It's a prayer he has never once refused."

"*If you are there,*" the boy whispered, "*let me know you.*"

"Yes."

They said nothing else for a long time. The rain had stopped. Through the gap between the boards, if he turned his head a little, Yoichi could see a thin line of sky, and in it, one star.

Toward morning, the boy heard him speak again, so softly that he had to put his ear against the boards. It was not to him. It was not in any language Sukezō knew. Two words, and then, after a while, the same two again, very softly, as if someone had called him by his name from the next room and he was answering.

Then there was nothing.

* * *

When the officials came up the hill in the morning they hauled him out of the pit and laid him on the wet grass, and the doctor bent over him and stood up again and shook his head. They wrote in the register: *Yoichi, turnkey, of Kōya-machi. Did not recant. Died in the pit on the fourth day.*

They burned his body on the hill, as was the custom, and took the ashes out in a boat beyond the harbor mouth and scattered them on the sea, so that no one could gather them up.

The ward elder, Shōzaemon, was reprimanded for laxity in the oversight of his ward and fined a sum of silver that he could ill afford. The officials came back to Kōya-machi, and every household stepped again, before them, one by one, on the brass. Every one of them stepped. Osen held Okiku very tightly in her arms, and set her down on the face, and lifted her off it, and did not let her look.

The examiners sent inquiries out along the north road, and through the villages beyond the city, but they had nothing to go on but a direction and the shrugs of Yoichi's neighbors, and after a while the matter was closed. No one came to Urakami. In the spring, Matakichi planted the rice.

It was Sukezō who was given Yoichi's padded winter coat to burn, with the rest of his things. He took it behind the guardroom to the fire. He found the paper inside the lining when he felt something stiff under his hand, and he unfolded it, and saw the close careful brushwork, and read the first line, and folded it up again very quickly, with his heart pounding, and put it inside his own coat. And then he burned the rest.

He could hardly read. He was a ward runner's son, and had had only two winters at the temple school. It took him half a year to read it all, by himself, alone, at night, a few characters at a time.

*O Deus, who are infinitely good, and worthy to be loved above all things.*
