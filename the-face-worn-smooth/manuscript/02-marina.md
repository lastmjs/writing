# II. Marina
## 祖母 · The same winter

His grandmother lived with him in two rooms at the back of a tenement row in Kōya-machi, the dyers' ward, where the gutters ran blue and red and yellow after rain and the whole lane smelled of indigo vats. She was nearly seventy. She had raised him since he was four, after the fever that took his mother and father in the same week, and she had done it alone, with her needle and her small bad-tempered loom and a stubbornness that the neighbors spoke of with respect and some fear.

Her name was Toku. It was the name on the temple register at Daion-ji, and the name the ward elder called her by, and the name Yoichi had always known.

He came home on his rest day with a parcel of dried sardines and a headache, and he sat by the brazier while she worked at her loom, and after a while he told her about the prisoners from Kōri. He did not mean to tell her so much. But she asked questions, sharp ones, without looking up from the shuttle, and one answer led to another, and before he knew it he was telling her about the old man and the ladle, and the singing at night, and the words. *He has already been stepped on. For me.*

The shuttle stopped.

He looked up. His grandmother was sitting very still at the loom with her hands in her lap. Her face, in the light from the paper window, had gone the color of rice paste.

"Grandmother?"

"Go and buy oil," she said. "The lamp is nearly dry."

"It's full. I filled it this morning."

"Then go and buy charcoal."

He went. When he came back, she was lying on her quilt with her face to the wall, and she did not speak to him again that night.

* * *

It was three days before she told him. He had come home late through the sleet, soaked to the skin, and she had made him take off his wet clothes and sit wrapped in a quilt by the brazier while she heated broth. When he had drunk it, she sat down across from him, close, and took his hand in both of hers. Her hands were dry and light as old leaves.

"Listen to me," she said, "and do not say anything until I have finished. Not anything. Do you understand?"

He nodded.

"When I was a girl," she said, "this city was Christian. All of it. You have heard me say so, and you have heard me say it was madness. That is what we were taught to say afterward. I have said it so many times that some days I have believed it." She drew a breath. "I was baptized when I was a baby, in the church of Todos os Santos, up the hill where Shuntoku-ji is now. My mother carried me there. The priest poured water on my head and gave me a name. The name was Marina."

Yoichi opened his mouth. She tightened her hands on his, and he closed it again.

"I learned the prayers as a child from the *Dochirina*. That was a little book the padres printed, with questions and answers. *What does it mean to be a Kirishitan? It means to be a person of Kirishito, who holds his teaching in the heart and confesses it with the mouth.* I can still say all of it. Isn't that strange? I cannot remember what I ate the day before yesterday, but I can still say all of it." She laughed, a small, broken sound. "I married your grandfather in the church of Santo Domingo, which stood where the Daikan's house is now. We had your father. We had a daughter after him, who died. And then the persecution began."

She told him the rest quickly, as if it were a splinter she was pulling out. How the churches came down one by one. How the priests were sent away on ships and some of them came back in secret, and were caught, and burned. How the officials went from house to house with lists, and men lost their posts, and families were thrown out of their wards. How, in the end, there was a new magistrate who did not merely arrest people, but took them to the boiling springs at Unzen, up in the mountains, and poured the water on them by the ladleful until they gave up their faith or died.

"There was a woman in our lane," his grandmother said. "Her name was Isabel. She was my friend from the time we were girls. She had a laugh like a boy's, and she was always in trouble with the priests for talking in church. In the sixth year of Kan'ei, the officials brought a picture to our ward and laid it on the ground in front of the ward elder's house. It was a paper picture then, not a board. Of the Mother of God holding the child. And they told us to step on it, one by one, all of us. Isabel would not. She knelt down in front of it, there in the street, in front of everyone, and she put her hands together. They took her to Unzen. I never saw her again."

She was silent for a moment. The sleet hissed on the roof.

"And I stepped on it," she said. "I want you to know that. I put my foot on the face of the Mother of God and I walked over it into the ward elder's house and I wrote my name in his book. Your father was standing behind me. He was nineteen. He stepped after me. Because I had. And after that I took us both to Daion-ji and had us written in the register, and I never said the prayers again, not once, not even in my head, for twenty-eight years. I was afraid. That is all. There is no other reason. I was afraid."

Yoichi looked at her. Her eyes were dry. He had never seen her weep, not when his parents died, not ever.

"Why are you telling me this?" he said.

"Because of what your old man said." Her voice dropped very low. "*He has already been stepped on.* I had forgotten. Can you believe it? I had forgotten that part. All these years I have thought of the board as a thing that accused me. And it is true, it does. But I had forgotten that he let it happen. That he let them do everything to him. That he was stepped on first. Before me. For me."

And then, at last, she wept.

* * *

He should have told her to stop. He knew that. If anyone heard, if a neighbor with a grudge put an ear to the thin wall, if she let something slip to the ward elder's wife in her muddled way, it would mean the prison for both of them. He of all people knew what that meant.

He did not tell her to stop.

Instead, over the long nights of that winter, by the light of the brazier, with the shutters closed and the quilts hung over the wall that they shared with the dyer's family, Yoichi's grandmother taught him what she remembered.

She told him about Deus, who made heaven and earth and everything in them out of nothing, and who made people to know him and love him forever. She told him how people turned away from Deus and fell into sin and death, all of them, everywhere, and could not save themselves. She told him how Deus did not abandon them, but became a man himself, born of a poor young woman named Maria in a stable in a far country, and was called Zezus, and was the Kirishito, the anointed one. How he healed the sick and touched lepers and ate with outcasts and told stories about lost sheep and lost coins and a father who ran down the road to meet his worthless son. How the great men of his own country hated him and gave him to the foreigners who ruled them, and the foreigners whipped him and mocked him and crowned him with thorns and nailed him to a cross outside the walls of their city, and he died there.

"And this is the thing you must understand," she said. "He could have stopped them. He was Deus. He could have stopped all of it with one word. He did not. He let them kill him, because that was the price of our sin, and he paid it himself, so that we would not have to. He was stepped on for us. And then on the third day he rose up from the dead. And he is alive. He is alive *now*, Yoichi. In Paraiso, at the right hand of the Father. And he will come again."

She taught him the prayers. The *Ave Maria* first, because it was short. Then the *Credo*, which was long and full of strange words she could not explain. And then the prayer the Lord himself had taught, which she said in the old way she had learned it from the *Dochirina* as a child, in a whisper, with her eyes shut:

> *Ten ni mashimasu warera ga on-oya,*
> *mina wo tattomare tamae.*
> *Miyo kitari tamae.*
> *Ten ni oite oboshimesu mama naru gotoku,*
> *chi ni oite mo arase tamae...*

*Our Father, who art in heaven, may thy name be revered. May thy reign come. As thy will is done in heaven, so let it be done on earth also.*

"*Forgive us our trespasses*," she said, "*as we forgive those who trespass against us.* That is the hardest line. The padres used to say so. That is the line that will take you your whole life to learn."

He learned the words in a week. It was the other thing, the thing beneath the words, that he did not know how to learn. He asked her once how she could be sure it was true, any of it. Twenty-eight years she had not prayed. How could she believe it now?

His grandmother thought about it for a long time.

"I don't know how to answer that," she said finally. "I only know that when you told me what that old man said, it was like hearing my own name spoken by someone in the next room. Someone I thought was dead." She looked at him. "Go and ask him. Your Kyūemon. Ask him how he knows. He has more right to an answer than I have."
